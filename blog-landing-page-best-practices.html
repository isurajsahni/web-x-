Landing page best practices: the 11 elements every high-converting page shares

Most landing pages don’t fail because the offer is weak. They fail because the page argues with itself — too many messages, too many exits, too much friction between “interested” and “done.” Here’s what the high performers do differently.

Type “landing page best practices” into Google and you’ll find a hundred lists, most of them the same fifteen tips reshuffled. That’s not because the advice is wrong — it’s because a handful of things really do decide whether a page converts, and everyone eventually lands on the same handful. The trouble is knowing which ones matter for your page, in what order, and how far to take each one before you’re just adding clutter.

We build and audit landing pages for founders running paid campaigns, product launches and lead-gen funnels, and the pattern repeats: the pages that convert aren’t the prettiest ones. They’re the ones that make exactly one argument, remove every reason to hesitate, and load before the visitor’s patience runs out. If you’re deciding whether you even need a dedicated page versus sending traffic to your homepage, read landing page vs website: which do you actually need first. If the page already exists and you’re trying to work out why it isn’t converting, this is the checklist we run through: eleven elements, grouped into the eight moves below, followed by a side-by-side comparison of landing pages, homepages and websites, a real example and a prioritised fix list.

In one line

A high-converting landing page makes one promise to one audience and removes everything — every link, field, distraction and delay — that sits between the click and the conversion.

1. Start with one goal, not eleven

Before a single word of copy gets written, decide the one action this page exists to produce — a booked call, a free trial signup, a download, a purchase. Not three actions with equal billing. One. Every element on the page should earn its place by moving the visitor toward that single outcome; anything that doesn’t is a distraction, however useful it feels. This is the single biggest structural difference between a landing page and a normal website page, and it’s worth deciding on paper before you open a design tool.

2. Match the message, or lose the click

The headline visitors see on your page should feel like a continuation of the ad, email or search result they just clicked — same promise, same language, same offer. When the page says something even slightly different, the brain registers a mismatch and the instinct is to leave, not to read further and reconcile it. Message match is unglamorous, invisible when done right, and one of the most reliable levers for lifting conversion rate without touching design at all.

3. Lead with a benefit, not a slogan

A landing page headline has one job: answer “what do I get, and why should I care” in under two seconds. Clever wordplay and brand slogans lose to plain, specific benefit statements almost every time on a page whose only visitors are people who already clicked something to get there — they’re primed for the payoff, not a puzzle. Write the honest version of what you offer first; polish the wording after, without losing the clarity.

4. Make the top of the page do the work

Everything a visitor needs to decide “this is for me” — headline, one supporting line, a visual, and the primary call to action — should be visible without scrolling, on mobile as well as desktop. Below that, visual hierarchy should guide the eye in one direction: headline, supporting proof, then the button, with size, colour and whitespace doing the steering rather than the visitor having to hunt. The call to action should be the most visually prominent thing on the page — not the logo, not a hero photo, not a navigation bar you probably shouldn’t have included at all.

5. Prove it before they scroll past

Claims are cheap; proof is what actually reduces hesitation. Testimonials, client logos, review counts or a short case study all do the same job — they tell a sceptical visitor that people like them already took the risk and it worked out. Placement matters as much as the proof itself: put it near the headline for a first-impression lift, and repeat it near the form or button, right where hesitation peaks and a visitor is deciding whether to commit.

6. Every form field is a small tax on intent

A form is the last obstacle between interest and conversion, and each additional field is a small toll the visitor has to decide is worth paying. Ask for what you genuinely need to follow up well, and defer everything else to a later step in the relationship — a qualifying call, an onboarding form, a CRM enrichment tool. Cutting a form down to the essentials is consistently one of the cheapest, fastest wins available on an underperforming page; we cover the maths behind this, and the other levers that move conversion rate, in what actually moves your landing page conversion rate.

  • Ask only for what you’ll genuinely use in the first follow-up.
  • Use a single, benefit-driven button label — not a generic “Submit.”
  • Repeat the same CTA at the top and bottom of a longer page, worded identically.
  • Remove the main navigation menu; a landing page shouldn’t offer an exit route.
  • Show what happens immediately after the click — a confirmation, a next step, a timeframe.
The page that converts best is rarely the most creative one. It’s the one with the fewest reasons left to say no.

7. Speed and Core Web Vitals decide who even sees your headline

None of the above matters if the page hasn’t finished loading by the time an impatient visitor gives up and hits back. Paid traffic is especially unforgiving here — you’ve already paid for the click, and a slow page burns that spend for nothing. Compress images, defer anything non-essential, and keep the page as close to a single clean layout as possible rather than a stack of heavy scripts and widgets. We go deeper on why this affects both conversions and rankings in Core Web Vitals: how site speed decides ranking.

8. Design for the thumb, then test everything

For most campaigns, mobile is where the majority of clicks land, so the mobile layout isn’t a scaled-down version of the desktop page — it’s the primary design, with the desktop version adapted from it. Buttons need real tap targets, forms need to work with an on-screen keyboard without hiding the field being filled, and the whole page needs to feel deliberate on a small screen, not squeezed. Once the fundamentals above are in place, stop guessing and start testing — one headline against another, one CTA wording against another, one form length against another. Change a single variable at a time, give it enough traffic to mean something, and let the data settle arguments that opinions never will.

Landing page vs website vs homepage

Half the landing pages we audit aren’t really landing pages - they’re homepages with an ad pointed at them. The three do different jobs, and knowing which one you’re building decides almost every best practice above:

  • A website is the whole collection of pages about your business: services, about, blog, contact, case studies. It serves every kind of visitor.
  • A homepage is the front door of that website. It introduces the business and routes each visitor to the right section, so it has to offer many paths.
  • A landing page is a standalone page built for one campaign, one audience and one action. It deliberately offers only one path.

A real example: how these principles shaped the Canvas storefront

Best practices are easiest to judge on a live project. Our Canvas case study is a luxury canvas and nameplate brand whose product sells on feel, so the site had to do the work a showroom normally does. It’s a storefront rather than a single campaign page, but the same principles decided the build:

  • One idea above the fold. The positioning line - Right to Luxury - leads, with the product given room instead of competing banners.
  • Visual hierarchy that points one way. Generous space and large art-directed imagery guide the eye to the product, then to the next step.
  • Speed without sacrificing imagery. Every image ships as modern AVIF, lazy-loaded and correctly sized, so an image-heavy page stays fast - the Core Web Vitals discipline from point 7.
  • A checkout that gets out of the way. The fewest possible steps between interest and purchase, the ecommerce version of cutting form fields.

If you’re planning campaign pages for a store like this, the landing pages sit alongside the website: each ad gets a focused page, and the storefront handles everyone else.

How to improve landing page conversion rate: a prioritised checklist

If your page already exists and isn’t converting, don’t redesign it first. Work down this list in order - the early fixes are cheap and usually have the biggest effect:

  1. Check message match. Put your ad and your headline side by side. If the promise differs, fix the headline today.
  2. Remove exits. Take out the navigation menu, footer links and any second call to action.
  3. Cut form fields. Remove every field you won’t use in the first follow-up.
  4. Fix mobile speed. Test on a real phone over mobile data. Compress images and remove heavy scripts until it loads quickly.
  5. Move proof next to the button. Put a testimonial, client logo or result right beside the form.
  6. Rewrite the button label. Replace “Submit” with the benefit: “Get my quote”, “Book my call”.
  7. Only then, test. Run A/B tests on the headline and offer once the basics are fixed and you have enough traffic.

What counts as a “good” rate depends heavily on industry, offer and traffic source. We give benchmarks by page type and a full testing playbook in how to increase landing page conversion rate.

Common landing page mistakes

  • Sending ad traffic to the homepage. The visitor has to find the offer again, and many won’t.
  • One page for every campaign. Different audiences need different headlines. Build a page, or at least a variant, per campaign.
  • Hiding the price or next step. If people have to guess what happens after they click, many won’t click.
  • Stock proof. Generic stock photos and vague claims reduce trust rather than build it. Use real work and real names.
  • Testing too early. A/B testing a slow, cluttered page tells you which version of a broken page is less broken.
  • No tracking. Without conversion tracking connected to the ad platform, you can’t tell which campaign or page is working.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best practices for a landing page?
Give the page one goal, match the headline to the ad that sent the visitor, lead with a clear benefit, keep the offer and call to action above the fold, show proof near the button, ask for as few form fields as possible, load fast on mobile, remove navigation, and test changes one at a time.
How can I improve my landing page conversion rate?
Start with the cheap fixes: match the headline to your ad, remove the navigation and extra calls to action, cut form fields, speed the page up on mobile, and put proof next to the button. Once those are done and you have steady traffic, A/B test the headline and offer.
What is the difference between a landing page and a website?
A website is the full set of pages about your business and serves every kind of visitor. A landing page is a single standalone page built for one campaign, one audience and one action, usually with no navigation. Most businesses need a website and use landing pages for paid campaigns.
What is the difference between a landing page and a homepage?
A homepage is the front door of your website: it introduces the business and routes different visitors to different sections, so it offers many paths. A landing page serves one audience arriving from a specific ad or link and offers exactly one path, one call to action.
Should a landing page have a navigation menu?
Usually not. Every link in a menu is a way to leave without converting. Remove the main navigation and footer links, keep your logo unlinked or linked only to the same page, and keep one call to action repeated down the page.
How long should a landing page be?
As long as the decision needs. A free download or simple sign-up can convert on a short page. A higher-priced service or product needs more explanation and proof, so a longer page often works better. Whatever the length, the offer and call to action should be visible without scrolling.
How long does it take to build a landing page?
A landing page designed and built from scratch usually takes 1–2 weeks, including copy, design, build and tracking setup. If the design is already finished in Figma, building it on Unbounce, Webflow or Framer can take just a few business days.
