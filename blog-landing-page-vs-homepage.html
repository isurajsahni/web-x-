Landing page vs homepage: what’s the difference, and when to use each

Your homepage is built to serve everyone who might ever do business with you. A landing page is built to serve one person, arriving for one reason, with one decision to make. Confuse the two and you pay for traffic that has nowhere useful to land.

By Web{X} Studio·Aug 2026·8 min read

Every business we build for eventually asks some version of the same question: “Should this campaign point to a landing page, or just to our homepage?” It sounds like a small technical decision. It isn’t. It’s the difference between a marketing budget that compounds and one that quietly evaporates.

The confusion is understandable, because a homepage and a landing page can look alike - both are web pages, both have a headline, an image, a button. But they’re built to answer a completely different question. A homepage answers “who are you, and what do you do?” for a stranger who could be anyone. A landing page answers “should I do the one thing this page is asking me to do?” for a visitor you already know something about, because you’re the one who sent them there.

In one line

A homepage serves everyone - every audience, every intent, every stage of interest. A landing page serves one audience, on one campaign, toward one action. That single difference decides everything else about how each page should be built.

Who each page is actually for

Your homepage has to work for a first-time visitor from Google, a returning customer checking your latest work, a journalist looking for your press page, a candidate checking if you’re hiring, and a competitor doing recon - often within the same hour. It can’t assume much about any of them, so it has to offer something for all of them: a summary of who you are, a taste of what you do, and enough doors that everyone can find the one they need.

A landing page has the opposite job. It exists because you already know exactly who is arriving - you sent the ad, wrote the email, or ran the campaign that put them there. That certainty is what lets you strip everything else away. You’re not guessing what this visitor wants; you already told them what they’d get if they clicked, and the landing page’s only job is to deliver on that promise and ask for the next step.

Where the traffic comes from

This is usually the clearest signal for which page you need. Homepages are built for traffic that arrives with an open-ended intent: organic search, direct visits to your domain, referrals, people typing your brand name because someone mentioned you at dinner. That traffic hasn’t committed to anything yet, so the homepage’s job is exploration and trust.

Landing pages are built for traffic that arrives with a pre-made promise attached: a paid ad, an email link, a QR code on a flyer, a social post with a specific offer. The visitor has already read a headline and clicked because of it. If the page they land on doesn’t continue that exact conversation, the trust breaks immediately - and so does the conversion.

Navigation: the quiet decision that changes everything

A homepage needs full navigation. Services, work, about, blog, contact - visitors should be able to wander toward whatever answers their specific question, because you don’t know what that question is yet. Removing navigation from a homepage would be actively hostile to the people it’s meant to serve.

A landing page needs the opposite. Every link that isn’t the one conversion action is a door out of the room you’re trying to hold the visitor in. That’s why well-built landing pages strip the main menu entirely, or reduce it to a single anchor link back to the offer. It isn’t laziness - it’s intentional. Fewer choices, more conversions. We cover the mechanics of this in landing page best practices, but the short version is: a homepage is designed to be left in every direction, and a landing page is designed to be left in exactly one.

The mistake we see most often

A founder runs a paid campaign, writes a sharp ad promising one specific outcome, gets a healthy click-through rate - and points every click at the homepage. The visitor lands, sees a full menu, a hero slider, three different calls to action and a blog link, and has to do the work of finding the thing the ad promised. Most won’t bother. They bounce, the ad spend is gone, and the founder concludes the campaign “didn’t work,” when really the page just wasn’t built to receive that traffic.

Send that identical click to a dedicated landing page that repeats the ad’s exact headline and offer, with one button and nothing competing for attention, and conversion rates commonly double or triple on the same budget. Nothing about the audience changed. Only the page they landed on did.

A homepage is where people go to learn who you are. A landing page is where you send people you’ve already told what they’ll get.

Metrics: you’re not measuring the same thing

Judging a homepage by conversion rate alone misreads its job - a visitor researching three vendors before choosing one isn’t a failed conversion, they’re doing exactly what a homepage exists for. Better homepage signals are engagement (time on site, pages per visit), search visibility, and whether visitors find their way to the right deeper page.

A landing page has one honest scoreboard: did the visitor complete the one action it asked for, yes or no. Everything on the page - copy, layout, image, button colour - exists to move that single number. That clarity is also why landing pages are so easy to A/B test and so hard to argue with once the data comes in.

Why you need both, not either

The two pages aren’t competing for the same job - they’re doing two different jobs that both matter. Your homepage is the permanent, evolving hub that carries your brand, earns search rankings over years, and gives every kind of visitor a place to start. Your landing pages are disposable, purpose-built tools you spin up and retire as campaigns come and go. A business with only a homepage struggles to convert paid traffic efficiently. A business with only landing pages has nowhere for organic search, referrals, or repeat visitors to go. If you’re weighing which single page to build first for a new venture, that’s a related but different question - we walk through it in landing page vs website: which do you actually need.

A simple rule of thumb

  • If you’re paying for the click, send it to a landing page.
  • If the visitor found you on their own, your homepage should be ready to receive them.
  • If the message on the page doesn’t match the message that got them to click, you’ve used the wrong page.
  • If you’re unsure, ask what the visitor already believes about you the moment they arrive - that answer tells you which page you need.

What this means for your budget

Building a dedicated landing page for every campaign sounds like extra cost, but the maths usually runs the other way. A homepage that’s doing double duty as a conversion page for paid traffic is a homepage that’s either too cluttered to convert or too stripped-down to properly represent your brand to organic visitors - you end up under-serving both jobs at once. A focused landing page, even a simple one, typically pays for itself within the first campaign through a higher conversion rate alone.
