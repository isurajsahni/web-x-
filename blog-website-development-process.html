The website development process, step by step

Great websites aren’t improvised - they’re built through a repeatable process. Here are the seven stages that take a project from a rough idea to a fast, ranking, launched website, and what happens (or goes wrong) at each one.

Ask ten agencies how they build a website and you’ll get ten answers - but the good ones all follow a version of the same seven stages. Understanding this website development process helps you brief better, spot a studio that skips steps, and know exactly where your project is at any moment.

At Web{X} Studio this is how every build runs, whether it’s a landing page or a full ecommerce store. Below you’ll find the process as a flowchart, each stage with what you should receive at the end of it, a realistic week-by-week timeline, and the mistakes that cause most delays. If you’re new to the topic, start with what website development is.

The 7 stages at a glance

Discovery → Design → Front-end development → Back-end & CMS → Content & SEO → Testing & QA → Launch & maintenance. Miss one and it usually resurfaces later as a delay, a bug, or a page that won’t rank.

The website development process flowchart

Each box is a stage with a clear output. You sign off that output before the next stage starts, which is what stops a project drifting.

  1. Discovery & strategyOutput: a written brief, sitemap, scope and fixed quote.
  2. UX & UI designOutput: wireframes, then approved high-fidelity designs in Figma.
  3. Front-end developmentOutput: responsive pages on a private preview link.
  4. Back-end & CMSOutput: working forms, integrations and an editor your team can use.
  5. Content & SEOOutput: final copy and media in place, meta data and structured data set.
  6. Testing & QAOutput: a tested site with speed, accessibility and device checks passed.
  7. Launch & maintenanceOutput: a live site, analytics and Search Console connected, a support plan.

The 4 phases of website development

You’ll also see the process described as four phases rather than seven steps. It’s the same work grouped differently:

PhaseStages it coversThe question it answers
1. PlanDiscovery & strategyWhat are we building, for whom, and why?
2. DesignUX & UI designWhat will it look like and how will people use it?
3. BuildFront-end, back-end & CMS, content & SEOHow do we make it work, fast and findable?
4. Launch & growTesting & QA, launch & maintenanceIs it ready, and how do we keep it improving?

Whether a proposal lists four phases, five stages or seven steps doesn’t matter. What matters is that every piece of work above appears somewhere in it. The next sections go through the seven steps one at a time.

Step 1 - Discovery & strategy

Before a single pixel or line of code, you define the why: business goals, target audience, scope, sitemap and how success will be measured. This is the cheapest place to make changes and the most expensive place to skip. A clear brief here prevents the painful rework of building the wrong thing beautifully.

Good discovery produces a document you can read in ten minutes: who the site is for, what each page must do, which features are in and out of scope, the platform decision (custom code, Webflow, WordPress, Shopify) and a fixed price. If you can’t explain the project in a paragraph after this stage, it isn’t finished.

Step 2 - UX & UI design

Next, the site is designed - usually in Figma. Wireframes map the structure and user journey; high-fidelity designs add brand, typography, colour and motion. This is where UI/UX decisions that drive conversions get made. Sign-off here means everyone agrees what’s being built before development starts.

Ask to see mobile designs, not only desktop - most visitors will arrive on a phone. And ask how hover states, empty states and error messages will look, because these are the gaps that slow development later. If you already have a finished design, you can skip straight to the build with a Figma-to-Webflow style service.

Step 3 - Front-end development

Now the design becomes a real, responsive interface - built with clean HTML, CSS and JavaScript, or on a visual platform. Good front-end work is pixel-perfect to the design, fast on mobile, and accessible. This is where a great design either comes to life or gets flattened by sloppy build.

You should get a private preview link early in this stage, not at the end. Reviewing real pages on your own phone surfaces problems a design file never will, and it’s far cheaper to adjust now than after content is loaded.

Step 4 - Back-end & CMS

For anything beyond a static page, the back-end wires up the logic: databases, user accounts, payments, integrations, and a content management system so you can edit the site without a developer. The more your site does, the bigger this stage. A brochure site may need a day here; an ecommerce store or a web app can spend weeks.

Step 5 - Content & SEO

A website without content is an empty shell. Final copy, images and video go in - each image optimised, each page given a unique meta title, description and structured data. This is where SEO is baked in, not bolted on: clean headings, internal links and fast media decide whether you rank. Unready content, by the way, is the single most common cause of delayed launches.

If you’re redesigning an existing site, this stage also includes a redirect map: every old URL pointed to its new equivalent with a 301, so the rankings you’ve already earned carry over instead of disappearing on launch day.

Step 6 - Testing & QA

Before launch, everything is tested: across browsers and devices, for broken links, form failures, accessibility and - crucially - speed. A quality studio checks Core Web Vitals here and fixes anything that fails, because a slow site that “works” still loses customers and rankings.

A practical QA pass covers: every form submitted and received, every link clicked, every page loaded on a real phone over mobile data, keyboard navigation, image alt text, and a check that the site isn’t accidentally blocked from search engines.

Step 7 - Launch & maintenance

Launch is a milestone, not the finish line. The site is deployed, analytics and Google Search Console are connected, the sitemap is submitted, and redirects are live so no SEO is lost. Then comes maintenance: security updates, backups, edits and ongoing improvements. A website is a living asset - the ones that keep winning are the ones that keep getting looked after.

A good process doesn’t make websites slower to build - it makes them faster to build right, the first time.

Website development timeline: how long each stage takes

Roughly: a landing page takes 1-2 weeks, a business website 3-6 weeks, an ecommerce store 6-10 weeks, and a custom web app 3-6 months or more. Here is how a typical 3-6 week business website splits across the stages:

StageTypical durationWhat you do in this stage
Discovery & strategy2-5 daysAnswer questions, share examples, approve the brief
UX & UI design1-2 weeksReview designs, give consolidated feedback
Front-end development1-2 weeksReview the preview link on your own devices
Back-end & CMS2 days - 1 weekProvide logins for payment, CRM and email tools
Content & SEORuns alongside the buildDeliver final copy and images
Testing & QA2-4 daysTest forms, approve for launch
Launch1 dayConfirm domain and email access

The biggest variable isn’t the studio - it’s how fast you supply content and feedback. We cover what speeds a project up and slows it down in how long website development takes.

What slows the process down (and how to avoid it)

  • Content arriving late. Start writing copy during design, not after the build. Placeholder text hides layout problems.
  • Feedback from too many people. Nominate one decision-maker and send consolidated feedback in one message per round.
  • Scope changes mid-build. New pages or features are fine, but add them as a second phase rather than reopening signed-off stages.
  • Skipping discovery. The projects that run longest are almost always the ones that started building before anyone agreed what to build.
  • Waiting on third parties. Payment gateway approval, domain access and hosting logins can each take days. Request them in week one.

How the process changes: custom code vs website builders

The seven stages stay the same whichever tool you build with, but their weight shifts. On a platform like Webflow, WordPress or Shopify, stage 4 (back-end) is much shorter because the platform provides hosting, a CMS and checkout. On a custom-coded site or web app, stage 4 is often the longest part of the project. DIY builders compress stages 2-4 into choosing and editing a template, which is quick but limits what the finished site can do. How to prepare before you hire a developer

You’ll get a better quote and a faster build if you arrive with:

  • The one or two actions the site must drive - calls, enquiries, sales, sign-ups.
  • Three to five websites you like, and what specifically you like about each.
  • A rough list of pages and any must-have features.
  • Your logo and brand files, or a note that you need branding too.
  • A budget range and a real deadline, with the reason behind it.

Frequently asked questions

What are the stages of website development?
The seven stages are: discovery and strategy, UX/UI design, front-end development, back-end and CMS setup, content and SEO, testing and QA, and launch and maintenance. Skipping any of them usually shows up later as delays, bugs or poor rankings.
What are the steps of the website development process?
Step 1 is discovery, where goals, scope and a sitemap are agreed. Step 2 is design in Figma. Steps 3 and 4 are front-end and back-end development. Step 5 adds content and SEO, step 6 is testing, and step 7 is launch followed by ongoing maintenance.
What are the phases of website development?
The process is often grouped into four phases: plan (discovery), design (UX and UI), build (front-end, back-end, content and SEO) and launch and grow (testing, launch and maintenance). It is the same work as the seven steps, grouped differently.
What is a typical web development timeline?
A landing page takes about 1–2 weeks, a business website 3–6 weeks, an ecommerce store 6–10 weeks, and a custom web app 3–6 months or more. The biggest variable is how quickly the client supplies content and feedback.
What is the most important stage of web development?
Discovery. A clear brief — goals, audience, scope and sitemap — prevents the expensive rework that comes from building the wrong thing. Great projects are won or lost before the first line of code.
What do I need to provide during website development?
Your goals and examples during discovery, consolidated feedback on designs, final copy and images during the build, and access to your domain, hosting and any payment or CRM tools before launch. Delivering content on time is the single biggest thing that keeps a project on schedule.
What happens after a website is launched?
Maintenance: security and platform updates, backups, content edits, monitoring speed and search performance in Google Search Console, and improving pages based on real visitor data. A website keeps earning only if it keeps being looked after.
