How to hire a web development, landing page & SEO agency as a US business (2026 guide)

Search “web development agency” or “SEO agency” in the US and you get thousands of options, from Manhattan firms billing $200 an hour to offshore studios billing a quarter of that. This guide explains what each one really costs, what good work looks like, and how to pick a partner without getting burned.

Most US businesses don’t actually need three agencies. They need a website that loads fast and explains the offer, landing pages that turn ad clicks into leads, and SEO that brings in customers who didn’t have to be paid for. Those three jobs overlap heavily, and the way you hire for them decides most of the result.

A quick disclosure: we’re a design and development studio based in Ludhiana, India, and we work with clients in the US. So we’re one of the options this guide compares. We’ve tried to write it so it’s useful even if you end up hiring someone down the street instead.

In one line

Hire for ownership, communication and proof, not location. A US agency makes sense when you need people in the room or onshore compliance. An offshore studio makes sense when you want senior design, development and SEO for a fixed price, and you’re comfortable working asynchronously.

What you’re actually hiring: three jobs, one outcome

Web development is the foundation: a custom-designed site on a platform your team can edit, such as Webflow, WordPress, Shopify or a custom stack. It needs to be fast and accessible, and structured so search engines understand it. See what that involves on our web development page.

Landing page design and development is the sharp end of paid marketing: one page, one offer, one action, built to match the ad that sent the visitor. It’s a different skill from building a website, because every element is judged by conversion rate. We break it down on our landing page design page and in landing page vs website.

SEO is what keeps the site found after launch: technical health, content that answers what buyers search, and the authority signals that help Google trust you.

Here’s why it matters who does all three. A slow page loses both rankings and conversions. A landing page with no indexable content can’t rank, and a well-ranked page that doesn’t convert is just traffic. When three vendors each own one piece, nobody owns the outcome. That’s the strongest argument for a single team that designs, builds and optimizes, whichever country it sits in.

US agency, freelancer or offshore studio: when each one makes sense

Choose a US agency if you need in-person workshops, you work in a regulated field that requires onshore staff or US-only data handling, or your project depends on deep local market knowledge that would be slow to hand over.

Choose a US freelancer if the job is small and well defined, such as a single landing page or a round of technical fixes, and you’re happy to be the project manager.

Choose an offshore studio if you want a full team (design, development, SEO and QA) for a fixed price. It also suits you if you’re comfortable briefing in writing and reviewing work asynchronously, and you’d rather put the savings into ads, content or a second phase.

The option to be wary of is the middle ground: a US-branded agency that quietly subcontracts everything offshore and marks it up. There’s nothing wrong with offshore delivery, but you deserve to know who’s building your site. Ask directly.

Time zones: how offshore teams really work with US clients

India is 9.5 hours ahead of the US East Coast during daylight saving time (10.5 in winter), and 12.5 hours ahead of the West Coast. People expect that to be a problem. In practice it often speeds things up.

  • Overnight progress. Feedback you send at the end of your day is worked on while you sleep, and you wake up to the revised design or a staging link.
  • One overlap window. A studio day in India usually ends around 9:30am Eastern, so a short morning call on the East Coast, or an early one on the West Coast, covers anything that needs a conversation.
  • Written-first process. Recorded walkthroughs, a shared project board and written decisions replace most meetings, and leave a paper trail you’ll be glad of later.

Before you sign, agree on the overlap window, the response time for messages and who your single point of contact is. Then check they stick to it during the first week.

What a good landing page agency delivers

If most of your budget goes to Google Ads or Meta, the landing page is where it’s won or lost. A good landing page partner should bring:

  • Message match. The headline repeats the promise of the ad or keyword that brought the visitor there, and each campaign gets its own variant.
  • One goal per page. A single call to action, no site navigation to leak visitors, and a form that asks only what sales actually needs.
  • Speed on mobile. The main content should load in under 2.5 seconds, which is Google’s “good” threshold for Largest Contentful Paint. Our Core Web Vitals guide explains why this affects both rankings and ad costs.
  • Tracking that works on day one. GA4 conversion events, ad platform pixels and consent handling set up and tested before launch.
  • A testing plan. Clear hypotheses for the first A/B tests, not just a nice design handed over.
  • The right platform. Unbounce, Webflow, WordPress or Shopify, chosen for how your team will run campaigns, not for what the agency happens to prefer.

For the full checklist, read landing page best practices and our conversion rate playbook. If you already have designs, a Figma to Unbounce or Figma to Webflow build is usually the fastest route.

What real SEO work looks like, and what it doesn’t

“SEO agency” covers everything from serious technical teams to people selling bulk backlinks. Real SEO work falls into four buckets, and a good agency can explain what it will do in each one every month:

  1. Technical: crawlability, site speed, structured data, canonical tags, redirects and fixing whatever blocks indexing.
  2. Content: service and location pages built around what US buyers search, plus guides that answer pre-purchase questions.
  3. Local: Google Business Profile optimization, consistent name, address and phone details, and a steady review process if you serve specific US cities or regions.
  4. Authority: earning genuine mentions and links through PR, partnerships and content people want to cite.

Google’s own guidance on hiring an SEO is clear that nobody can guarantee a top ranking. Treat any guarantee of “#1 on Google” as a reason to walk away. Also be wary of reports built only around rankings for keywords nobody searches. The numbers that matter are qualified traffic, leads and revenue.

US-specific requirements your agency should know

This is where offshore and out-of-state agencies most often slip. Whoever you hire should raise these without being prompted:

  • Accessibility (ADA). Website accessibility lawsuits are common in the US, and the working standard is WCAG 2.1 AA: color contrast, keyboard navigation, alt text, form labels and captions. Accessibility should be built in, not bolted on with an overlay widget.
  • Privacy laws. California’s CCPA/CPRA and a growing list of other state laws affect how you collect data, run tracking pixels and honor opt-outs. Your forms, cookie consent and privacy policy need to reflect where your customers live.
  • Consent for texts and calls. If your forms collect phone numbers for SMS or call follow-up, the consent wording matters under the TCPA. Get your counsel to approve it.
  • US conventions. US English spelling, USD pricing, MM/DD dates, US phone formats and address fields that fit US addresses. It sounds minor, but visitors notice.

We’re a studio, not a law firm, so treat the legal points as a checklist to confirm with your attorney, not as legal advice.

12 questions to ask before you sign

  1. Who exactly will design, build and optimize my site, and can I talk to them before signing?
  2. Can you show me live sites you built, and put me in touch with a client I can call?
  3. Is the price fixed, and what triggers a change order?
  4. Will my domain, hosting, analytics and ad accounts be in my name from day one?
  5. Does the contract assign all intellectual property to me on final payment?
  6. What are the milestones, and what do I pay at each one?
  7. What is your overlap window with my time zone, and your response time for messages?
  8. How do you handle accessibility and US privacy requirements?
  9. What Core Web Vitals scores will the site pass at launch?
  10. What SEO work is included at launch, and what is monthly?
  11. How will you report results, and which numbers tie back to revenue?
  12. If we part ways, what do I get, and how long does the handover take?

Red flags, wherever the agency is based

  • Guaranteed rankings, or “page one in 30 days”.
  • The agency registers your domain or hosting in its own name.
  • A portfolio of templates or mockups with no live sites you can visit.
  • Hourly billing with no cap on an open-ended scope.
  • 100% payment upfront on a project longer than a couple of weeks.
  • SEO packages sold by the number of backlinks.
  • No written scope, or a scope that says “website” and little else.
The cheapest agency is the one you only have to hire once. Pay for clarity, ownership and proof, and the location takes care of itself.

The honest takeaway

The right web development, landing page or SEO agency for a US business is the one that shows you real work, puts ownership in writing, agrees a fixed scope and communicates clearly across whatever distance sits between you. For some businesses that’s a firm across town. For many others, it’s a senior offshore team that frees up budget for the ads and content that actually grow the business.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe for a US business to outsource web development to an agency in India?
Yes, provided you protect yourself the same way you would with any vendor. Keep your domain, hosting, analytics and ad accounts in your own name, sign a contract that assigns the intellectual property to you on payment, pay against milestones rather than all upfront, and make sure the code lives in a repository or platform account you control. A good offshore studio will suggest all of this before you ask.
How much does an SEO agency cost per month in the USA?
US agency SEO retainers commonly fall somewhere between about $2,000 and $10,000 a month for a small or mid-sized business, and more for competitive national campaigns. Offshore teams usually quote a fraction of that for similar scope. The number matters less than what it buys: ask for a written monthly plan covering technical fixes, content and links, and reporting tied to leads or revenue rather than rankings alone.
How long does it take to design and build a landing page?
A single, focused landing page usually takes 3 to 5 business days from kickoff to launch once the copy, offer and brand assets are ready. Add time if the page needs original illustration, video, complex integrations, or a round of A/B test variants.
Should I hire one agency for web development, landing pages and SEO, or separate specialists?
For most small and mid-sized businesses, one team is simpler and usually produces better results, because site speed, page structure and content all affect both conversions and rankings. Separate specialists make sense when you have an in-house marketing lead who can coordinate them, or when one of the three jobs is large enough to need a dedicated team.
Can an offshore agency handle local SEO for US cities?
Yes. Local SEO is mostly done online: city and service pages, consistent name, address and phone details across directories, review generation, and optimizing your Google Business Profile. The profile should be owned by your business, and the agency is added as a manager. Where it needs local knowledge, such as neighborhoods, local terms and competitors, a good agency will ask you for it.
Who owns the website when the project is finished?
You should. Your contract should say that all design files, code and content transfer to you once the final invoice is paid, and the site should be hosted on accounts in your name. If an agency insists on keeping ownership or hosting the site on its own account with no exit plan, treat that as a red flag.
